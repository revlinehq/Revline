# Motion study: Instagram reel DfMzGyNBdUH

Source: https://www.instagram.com/reel/DfMzGyNBdUH/ (hc.prath). Study project: `j97dm1289746790x3aj8x7pey18hejvr`, development backend, timeline `main`. The source is 1080 × 1920, 25 fps, 12.821 s. It is a breakdown showing a monochrome storyboard alongside a finished vertical animation, over a dark editing-interface background. The finished animation is the right-hand panel; the left panel updates in parallel. The reference is in project media, not on the timeline.

## Evidence and precision

A 16-frame whole-film read was followed by consecutive reads at 10 samples/s across 0–12.8 s and 15 samples/s over the opening 0–0.6 s. The 10 Hz observations below locate changes within roughly 0.1 s, not exact source frames. Full-resolution `measureFrame` samples and `trackReference` fits are identified separately. The scene tracker follows image texture, not a semantic isolated layer; its estimated GSAP ease is a fit, not proof of the original curve. The source itself has 40 ms frame steps. `motionMap` sampled at 30 fps despite the 25 fps source, so its camera fit is also an estimate. No downscaled sheet was used for the pixel/color measurements.

## Motion mechanics and measurements

- **Type as a first impact:** from the 15 Hz read, “Anyone” is tiny at 0.00 s, already large at 0.067 s, then changes hue every ~0.07 s. At 0.40 s it is blue on a pale field for a very brief inversion. This is a sequence of discrete visual states, not a slow continuous rainbow blend. Each colored word preserves location, so color supplies the change without making the viewer reacquire the subject.
- **A moving anchor, then counter-motion:** `trackReference` on a 280 × 640 source-pixel box containing the hand and phone at 1.8 s fitted a move from center (790, 1022) to (787, 818), scale 1 → 0.777, over 1.84–2.40 s with `power1.out`, fit 0.92. The box includes both subject and background, so treat this as a tracked image-region move, not a measured independent hand rig. The edges' hands grow into frame while the central target travels upward and becomes smaller.
- **Chess staging in depth:** a 230 × 470 px box around the incoming pawn at 8.1 s fitted center (917, 885) → (861, 896), scale 1 → 0.782, over 8.14–8.42 s with `sine.out` (fit 0.90). A later fit at 8.58–8.82 s shrinks 0.782 → 0.647 with `sine.in` while moving down about 28 px. Those short changes separate arrival/settle from departure; do not treat the fit as proof that the source used that exact GSAP ease.
- **Layering and focus:** the image-cluster sequence gives nearby tiles larger sizes and moves them across a darker, blue-lit background while smaller tiles remain near the vanishing point. The chess sequence uses a perspective floor, frosted highlights, blurred distant pieces and a foreground pawn. The images do not establish a measured Z distance. A 2D scale plus position can suggest the first-order move, but accurate matching needs a real perspective or 3D scene and independent camera/object controls.
- **Cuts and transitions:** the strongest changes around 1.8, 3.5, 5.7, 7.2, 9.8 and 11.4 s change both storyboard and final-panel content together. The 4.4–4.6 s burst, and the 7.0–7.2 s push through the button, are designed visual handoffs rather than simple dissolves. Fast blur accompanies some scale moves, but the exact shutter or blur radius was not measured. No defensible numeric stagger, overshoot, mask geometry or morph duration could be extracted from these reads, so none is invented here.
- **Color samples, source pixels:** at 2.2 s the red panel reads `#bd1e1a` at (500,500) and `#cd1f1f` at (800,550); a border sample at (485,455) is `#e2e2e2`. At 8.5 s the blue background reads `#0046ba` at (550,500) and `#004fcd` at (900,600), with a light board sample `#d8e4ee` at (800,1050). At 10.5 s the yellow/orange field reads `#c0a543` at (550,500) and `#ba7417` at (1000,1300). These are individual compressed-frame pixels, not flat palette specifications.
- **Typography:** `matchFont` on “Final Video” proposed Comfortaa 700 (0.548), Unbounded 400 (0.524) and VT323 (0.503). Visual comparison shows the real title is pixel/block-like; the first two high scores are misleading and VT323 is still not an exact match. The matched crop reports ink `#f9f9f9` on `#080608`. “Taste” is contrasting light italic serif; the chess labels are light italic sans; the end card returns to pixel type. Font size and exact typeface remain unverified.

## Reusable design rules

1. Keep a stable outer frame while the idea inside changes. A fixed comparison layout can make very different visual styles feel like one coherent argument.
2. Turn each spoken phrase into a physical action, not a subtitle: generate becomes multiplication, choose becomes a press, judgment becomes a chess move. Anchor the action near the spoken word and allow the audience to register it.
3. Start with one word in one position; change its color or treatment in rapid discrete steps. Reserve a single field inversion as an accent, then move to the first concrete object.
4. Carry a silhouette, direction, scale or color into the next shot: the downward finger drives into a button, a tilted pawn becomes a broken diagonal form. Keep that match readable even if the background cuts.
5. Build depth with different scales and speeds for foreground and background. Let an object settle before accelerating it away, and use a short blur only on the fast part of the move.
6. Alternate information density: a rapid accumulation of options, then one hand; three quick chess ideas, then a nearly static orange hold. Use the hold to give a verbal point weight.
7. Repeat a motif in two visual languages when explaining a process. The storyboard and final image change together, so their relationship remains legible without additional labels.
8. Treat the last card as a reward: remove the complex environment, use one emblem and a two-level message, then hold long enough to read it.

## Revline findings and current build status

- `importVideoLink` rejected both the supplied Instagram URL and its canonical profile URL with “That link opens a webpage instead of an MP4.” A public local copy was obtained with `yt-dlp` and brought in through `uploadMedia`/`finishUpload`; that extra download is outside Revline. Native Instagram URL resolution with clear progress and fallback would remove this break. The downloader initially failed because its bundled Python could not load `pyexpat`; an existing compatible local Python was used for the download.
- `analyzeMedia(mode: reference)` failed: “Shot detection is not configured. Install the pinned Python worker dependencies on the server.” The frame reader still worked, but locating and numbering every cut became manual. A health check that identifies the missing worker dependency before a long analysis and a fallback cut detector would help.
- `readFrames`, source-space `measureFrame`, `trackReference` and the 15 fps `compareReference` sheet were useful together: they connect an actual crop, pixel coordinates and a timed motion fit. The tracker does not separate a subject from a changing background; semantic masks, confidence over the whole path, and multi-object tracking would make the reported ease safer to use.
- `listenReference` produced timestamps and levels but no sound descriptions. It cannot distinguish a spoken syllable, music beat and effect in this run. Stem-aware listening, waveform playback, beat confidence, and a visual timeline that aligns audio onsets with source frames would help.
- `findIcon(name: trophy, style: glyph)` returned no result and suggested tracing. `traceShape` returned a two-path, single-color threshold of the trophy crop, but merged highlights and omitted the other colors; it is not a usable faithful multicolor pixel trophy by itself. Layered color tracing and palette-aware pixel-art extraction would reduce manual reconstruction.
- `matchFont` ranked smooth Comfortaa above the visibly pixelated original. Character-shape and pixel-grid-aware ranking, plus a way to supply or identify missing fonts, would improve matching.
- The reference is a flattened composition containing a storyboard, photoreal hands, complex 3D objects and many distinct image cards. There is no automatic decomposition into independently editable layers, camera and 3D assets. A source-to-layer extraction workflow, with separate masks, inpainting and asset provenance, would speed recreations and also let designers study the original technique without putting the reference video on the timeline.
- A partial editable opening and generation beat were saved as one film clip with named markers. At revision 1, `getFrames` and `compareReference` show the colored-word timing but also wrong typography, a simplified background and storyboard, incorrect layout proportions, and a blank frame at 0.5 s. The 15 fps comparison scored 8.9–21.2% difference for sampled 0–0.467 s frames and 77.5% at 0.5 s. Scores are ranking aids, not proof of visual fidelity. `checkPacing` flagged a zoom ending without ease-out; reference pacing takes precedence only after a motion comparison confirms it. Revision 3 adds a generated computer and repeated image tiles from 0.5–1.76 s. The 15 fps comparison for 0.5–1.0 s returned 26.6–39.9% difference: the computer, cards and grayscale storyboard visibly differ from the source. Revision 6 replaces the computer with an isolated monitor and a drawn storyboard. A subsequent comparison returned “Comparison frames are waiting for a media worker. Try again shortly”; the revised frames and motion remain unverified. The remainder is blank. No completed recreation, sound mix, or full-film validation should be inferred from this partial build.
- Seven replacement stills were generated outside Revline using the built-in image generator, saved in [DfMzGyNBdUH-assets](./DfMzGyNBdUH-assets/), and uploaded to project media. They depict a phone held against red, a colorful computer, a blue glass pawn, a pointing hand, a broken glass pawn, an isolated monitor and a storyboard drawing. The prompts specified those observed subjects, framing and palettes, with no text; they are newly generated approximations, not source assets or editable 3D. All seven are attached to the film. The isolated monitor and storyboard appear in the second beat, with phone and chess stills appearing as tiles.
